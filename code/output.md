<h1>AI could create a perfect storm </h1><h1>of climate misinformation</h1>
<h1>We are in the midst of a transformation of the digital news ecosystem. </h1><h1>The expansion of online social networks, the influence of recommender </h1><h1>systems, increased automation, and new generative artificial intelligence </h1><h1>(AI) tools are rapidly changing the speed and the way misinformation </h1><h1>about climate change and sustainability issues moves around the world. </h1><h1>Policymakers, researchers and the public need to combine forces to </h1><h1>address the dangerous combination of opaque social media algorithms, </h1><h1>polarizing social bots, and a new generation of AI-generated content.</h1>
<p>When OpenAI released their version </p><p>of the artificial </p><p>intelligence  ChatGPT to the public in November 2022, </p><p>the playing ground for false news and misinformation </p><p>online changed completely. Suddenly, anyone could </p><p>with relatively little effort produce seemingly human-</p><p>written text and highly realistic images – regardless of </p><p>their truthfulness.</p>
<p>Misinformation and disinformation campaigns are not </p><p>new, and topics such as climate change have been in </p><p>the line of fire more than once. But over time, the </p><p>digital aspects of these phenomena have become </p><p>increasingly important for those interested in </p><p>understanding and tackling the harmful effects of mis- </p><p>and disinformation. </p>
<p>Information and communications technologies have </p><p>made the world increasingly connected, allowing </p><p>information to move almost effortlessly around the </p><p>world in the blink of an eye. What we see on our </p><p>screens everyday is increasingly determined by </p><p>recommender systems, systems designed to maximize </p><p>engagement, at times favoring whatever creates </p><p>engagement over truth. Sometimes, this information </p><p>movement is affected by social bots. Often, the </p><p>diffusion of information is shaped by how people </p><p>engage with it by sharing, commenting, remaking it and </p><p>moving it across digital media platforms and between </p><p>social and conventional media. This, in combination </p><p>with the capacities of generative AI to create synthetic </p><p>content, may very well result in a perfect storm of </p><p>misinformation. The time to act on these risks is now. </p>
<p>The following synthesis explores the increasing </p><p>influence of digital media, AI and algorithmic systems </p><p>on the creation, diffusion and amplification of </p><p>misinformation about climate and environmental </p><p>sustainability issues. We combine insights from various </p><p>research strands ranging from computational social </p><p>sciences, to sustainability sciences and neurosciences, </p><p>to explore what we know about how such information </p><p>is amplified, and to what extent recent advances in AI </p><p>pose new challenges for our ability to act collectively </p><p>on our planetary crisis. </p>
<p>This synthesis brief has been put together as an </p><p>independent contribution to the </p><p>Nobel Prize Summit </p><p>2023, “Truth, Trust and Hope”, Washington D.C., 24-26 </p><p>of May 2023.</p>







<h1>Box 1. Key terms</h1>

<h1>What is climate mis- and </h1><h1>disinformation – and why </h1><h1>should we care?</h1>
<h1>Our living planet is facing a multitude of interacting crises. Climate </h1><h1>change, the loss of biodiversity, inequality, pandemics, and war and </h1><h1>conflict are just a few of them. In these times of turbulence, it is vital that </h1><h1>decisions about our common future are well-grounded in science. False </h1><h1>information and intentional attempts to manipulate public opinion pose </h1><h1>serious risks to our joint capacities to create a safe and just future for all. </h1>
<p>Mis- and disinformation play a crucial</p><p> role in forming </p><p>public opinion and thus influencing the actions taken at </p><p>all levels of society, from individuals to central policy-</p><p>making. Misinformation about covid-19 vaccines for </p><p>example, has notable impacts on whether people are </p><p>willing to take the vaccine (Loomba et al., 2021). A </p><p>growing body of work also explores to what extent </p><p>misinformation has affected political elections around </p><p>the world (for example, Howard, 2020; Machado et al., </p><p>2019). Scientists engaged in science communication </p><p>also face an increased number of attacks in social </p><p>media as they struggle to inform the public to mitigate </p><p>the spread of fake news and misinformation (Nogrady, </p><p>2021). </p>
<p>It is becoming increasingly clear that misinformation is </p><p>taking its toll on public opinion on climate and </p><p>sustainability issues as well. A recent study shows that </p><p>U.S. citizens, partly due to biased and incorrect media </p><p>reporting, systematically underestimate public support </p><p>for ambitious climate policies, thus falsely assuming </p><p>that a vocal minority who dismiss climate change are </p><p>representative of the broader public opinion </p><p>(Sparkman et al., 2022). A cross-national study </p><p>conducted by the Climate Action Against </p><p>Disinformation and Conscious Advertising Network </p><p>(2022) shows a considerable spread of serious </p><p>misconceptions about climate change in the surveyed </p><p>countries. 33% of the surveyed population in the US </p><p>and Australia believe that ‘the climate has always </p><p>changed, global warming is a natural phenomenon and </p>
<p>is not a direct result of human activity.’ In Brazil, 30% </p><p>believe that climate change is not caused mainly by </p><p>human activity, and 24% believe that the “temperature </p><p>record is unreliable or rigged”. In the United Kingdom, </p><p>29% believe that a significant number of scientists </p><p>disagree on the cause of climate change (Climate </p><p>Action Against Disinformation and Conscious </p><p>Advertising Network, 2022). Misinformation and </p><p>conspiracy theories about wind energy farms are </p><p>already affecting the expansion of renewable energy </p><p>negatively, and thus the prospects for achieving a </p><p>transition to zero-carbon energy sources (Winter et al., </p><p>2022). </p>
<p>One key and challenging issue is how to define and </p><p>identify mis- and disinformation. Here we refer to </p><p>misinformation as information whose inaccuracy is </p><p>unintentional. Disinformation on the other hand, refers </p><p>to information that is deliberately false or misleading </p><p>(Jack, 2017). While the definitions of mis- and </p><p>disinformation might seem straightforward, their </p><p>differences in the real world are not (Jerit and Zhao, </p><p>2020; Treen et al., 2020).</p>
<p>Climate mis- and disinformation has a long history, and </p><p>its underlying motivations and strategies have become </p><p>increasingly visible in the last years (e.g., Supran et al., </p><p>2023; Franta, 2018). Attempts to sow confusion about </p><p>the science of climate change include lobbying </p><p>campaigns by vested interests (Brulle, 2018), the </p><p>funding of climate change denialist think tanks  </p>

<p>In parallel to this type of coordinated </p><p>disinformation activities there are other much more </p><p>fluid and complex self-organized patterns of </p><p>information sharing and engagements on digital </p><p>platforms. In the case of fossil fuel companies’ </p><p>documented attempts to influence public opinion </p><p>on climate change issues their agency is </p><p>straightforward (Supran and Oreskes, 2017; Supran </p><p>et al., 2023). But the type of mis-and disinformation </p><p>that flows effortlessly across social media platforms </p><p>like Twitter, TikTok and Youtube, is another type of </p><p>beast. The former has a clear intent, plan and </p><p>coordinating agent, while the latter form tends to </p><p>emerge and evolve as information, algorithmic </p><p>systems and digital social networks interact in </p><p>complex ways. These two forms of mis- and </p><p>disinformation are of course, often combined in </p><p>reality (Starbird and Wilson, 2019). </p>
<p>Digital media plays a key role in this regard (Pearce </p><p>et al., 2019; Treen et al., 2020). Five hundred million </p><p>tweets, 294 billion emails, 4 petabytes of content </p><p>on Facebook, 65 billion messages on WhatsApp, </p><p>and 5 billion searches on Google are conducted </p><p>every day (from Howard, 2020, p. 4). Social media </p><p>like Facebook, Twitter and Instagram is particularly </p><p>important in this context. Survey data shows that </p><p>between 40% and 60% of adults in most developed </p><p>countries, receive their news from social media </p><p>(Nic, 2017). The fact that social media platforms are </p><p>participatory by design allows them to shape </p><p>individual attitudes, feelings and behaviors </p><p>(Williams et al., 2015), and facilitates social </p><p>mobilization and protests (Steinert-Threlkeld et al., </p><p>2015). </p>
<p>Climate change and the loss of biodiversity require </p><p>collective action and public deliberations that are </p><p>global in scope, ambitious in its goals, and over very </p><p>extended periods of time (Galaz 2020). Polarization, </p><p>mistrust in science, and incorrect climate and </p><p>environmental information amplified through </p><p>digital media could undermine such much-needed </p><p>collective action in detrimental ways. </p>

<p>Why do people adopt beliefs,</p><p> such as that the earth is </p><p>flat, which are clearly incompatible with established </p><p>truth? Like with other beliefs, it depends on the nature </p><p>of their prior beliefs, goals, as well as learning and </p><p>reasoning processes. Our knowledge about the </p><p>psychological and neural mechanisms that promote </p><p>false beliefs is still nascent, but existing research in </p><p>psychology and neuroscience has described how </p><p>people process information that is unexpected, </p><p>emotional, and politically divisive – features typical of </p><p>false information encountered on social media.</p>
<p>First, information that is unexpected and emotionally </p><p>(especially negatively) loaded, tends to grasp our </p><p>attention, which depends on visual cortices and </p><p>attentional networks in parietal and frontal brain </p><p>regions. In addition, attention also depends on activity </p><p>in deep brain nuclei that are sensitive for events that </p><p>can be threatening and important goals of the </p><p>individual. </p>
<p>Various goals of the individual can be important. For </p><p>example, if social goals, such as promoting one’s own </p><p>social status or belonging to a particular group, are </p><p>more important than the goal to seek and represent </p><p>the truth, then attention is biased towards whatever </p><p>promotes the more important goals. Research has </p><p>shown that the orbitofrontal cortex, a brain region </p><p>located just behind the eyes, is critical for computing </p><p>the value of different goals. Other prefrontal regions </p><p>serve to monitor information that deviates from what </p><p>is in line with one’s own moral or political convictions.</p><p>People who strongly identify with different political </p><p>ideologies tend to distort their conclusions in line with </p><p>their political identities when asked to reason about </p>
<p>politically divisive facts. At first glance, one could think </p><p>that this is due to politically motivated reasoning. </p><p>However it can also be viewed as rational inferences </p><p>considering the individual’s prior belief system </p><p>(Botvinik-Netzer et al., 2023). Networks of brain </p><p>regions linked to memory retrieval and belief updating, </p><p>are likely to support these processes.</p>
<p>Recent research shows that simple learning </p><p>mechanisms, known to engage regions in the brain’s </p><p>system that run on dopamin, can explain how humans </p><p>are motivated to increase the consumption of </p><p>information that is liked by others – in particular by </p><p>those belonging to the same group as the individual </p><p>identifies with. For example, models of instrumental </p><p>learning explain why people engage with social media </p><p>(Lindström et al., 2021, Ceylan et al., 2023) and how </p><p>morally outraged messages become viral (Brady et al., </p><p>2021). <h1>The neuroscience of </h1><h1>false beliefs</h1>
<h1>What we believe is not only a result of our own reasoning, but also </h1><h1>of the beliefs of people around us. Our brains are wired to consume </h1><h1>information that is liked by our peers. That way, social reinforcers online </h1><h1>– in the form of likes, comments and shares – can build a basis for what </h1><h1>individuals believe to be true or false.</h1>

<p>media might thus provide a basis for what individuals </p><p>believe to be true. Indeed, research has shown that </p><p>social forms of learning can be at least as powerful, </p><p>and draw on many of the same neural principles as </p><p>learning based on own, personal experiences (Olsson </p><p>et al., 2020). The importance of understanding the </p><p>power of social influences is further underscored by </p><p>recent findings showing that the more a statement is </p><p>“liked” by others, the more the statement is </p><p>subsequently rated as true (Granwald et al., 2023).</p>

<h1>How algorithms diffuse and </h1><h1>amplify misinformation</h1>
<h1>Digital platforms are designed to maximize engagement. Recommender </h1><h1>systems play a key role as they shape digital social networks, and the </h1><h1>flow of information. Weaknesses in their underlying algorithmic systems </h1><h1>are already today being exploited to try to influence public opinion on </h1><h1>climate and sustainability issues. Automation through social bots also </h1><h1>play a role, although their impacts are contested. </h1>
<p>In 2010, a team of political scientists</p><p> in collaboration </p><p>with researchers at Facebook conducted a gigantic </p><p>experiment on the platform. It included “all users of at </p><p>least 18 years of age in the United States who </p><p>accessed the Facebook website on 2 November 2010, </p><p>the day of the US congressional elections”. This </p><p>61-million-person experiment tested whether a </p><p>simple tweak in the newsfeed of users – adding a “I </p><p>Voted” button and information about friends who had </p><p>voted – could impact voting behavior. </p>
<p>The results were clear: this small design change led to </p><p>higher real-world voting turnout. Facebook-users who </p><p>saw a message about one of their close friends on </p><p>Facebook having voted were 2.08% more likely to </p><p>vote themselves (Bond et al., 2012). </p>
<p>This controversial experiment illustrates a number of </p><p>issues that are key if we are to understand how digital </p><p>platforms interplay with the creation and diffusion of </p><p>mis- and disinformation, and their impacts. </p>
<p>First, digitalization and the expansion of social media </p><p>has not only fundamentally expanded the scale, but </p><p>also transformed the properties of social networks. </p><p>3,6 billion people use social media today. Through it, </p><p>they access a digital media environment where </p><p>language barriers slowly but surely are eroding as new </p><p>digital tools make communication across borders </p><p>increasingly frictionless. These new connections are </p><p>transforming the structures of social networks in ways </p><p>that allow for immediate communication across vast </p><p>geographical space (Bak-Coleman et al., 2021). </p>
<p>Second, the way information moves in these vastly </p><p>spanning digital social networks is not random. Its </p><p>movement is fundamentally affected by the way digital </p><p>media platforms such as recommender systems are </p><p>designed to maximize engagement and induce other </p><p>forms of behavioral responses amongst its users </p><p>(Kramer et al., 2014; Coviello et al., 2014). The </p><p>diffusion of such information can also be exploited by </p><p>external attempts to manipulate public opinion online. </p><p>Three common approaches include (1) specifically </p><p>targeting the way algorithmic systems operate, (2)</p><p>carefully crafting viral messages to sow confusion, and </p><p>(3) through the use of automation tools like ‘social bots’. </p>
<p>Social bots mimic human behavior online, and can be </p><p>used in ways to amplify certain types of information – </p><p>say, climate denialism –, or operate in ways that widen </p><p>social divisions online (Gorwa and Guilbeault, 2018; </p><p>Shao et al., 2018). Our own work (Daume et al., 2023) </p><p>shows that social bots play an unignorable role in </p><p>climate change conversation. They amplify information </p><p>that supports and opposes climate action at the same </p><p>time, especially information appealing to emotions, </p><p>such as sympathy or humor. </p>
<p>Recommender systems play a key role for the dynamics </p><p>of information diffusion and the evolution of digital </p><p>social networks (Narayanan, 2023), and they have </p><p>increasingly become infused with deep learning-based </p><p>AI (Engström and Strimling, 2020). People-</p><p>recommender systems for example (like “People You </p><p>May Know” on Facebook or “Who to Follow” on </p><p>Twitter) shape social network structures, thus </p>

<h1>Emotions and group dynamics </h1><h1>around misinformation on </h1><h1>social media</h1>
<h1>Nuanced views and clean facts don’t generate clicks. Social networks </h1><h1>are designed to speak to our emotions, and the more extreme the </h1><h1>emotions, the better the content. But that does not mean that good </h1><h1>arguments, education, and science communication are futile.</h1>
<p>Emotions attract our attention,</p><p> and provide us with </p><p>information about actions we should take: When you </p><p>feel fear, it’s best not to ignore the danger, and protect </p><p>yourself from it. When you are angry, it’s probably </p><p>because someone has treated you or a group you </p><p>belong to unfairly, and it’s time to step up against the </p><p>injustice. </p>
<p>News agencies, politicians, and creators of fake news </p><p>know this, and use it to create content that attracts </p><p>attention and is likely to be shared on digital and social </p><p>media. Algorithms on social media are optimized to </p><p>increase engagement with content (Narayanan, 2023; </p><p>Metzler & Garcia, 2022), and content that provokes </p><p>strong emotional reactions is a powerful means to do </p><p>so. Negative moral-emotional messages about groups </p><p>we do not like seem to particularly increase </p><p>engagement (Brady et al., 2017, Rathje et al. 2021, </p><p>Marie et al., 2023). Humans are social animals, and </p><p>things that make us feel part of a group, that increase </p><p>our group’s status, or decrease the status of an out-</p><p>group, are highly motivating for us (Robertson et al., </p><p>2022). </p>
<p>We can regularly observe such emotional group </p><p>dynamics around the topic of climate change on social </p><p>media. On the one hand, there are people who think we </p><p>are not doing enough and need to urgently take action: </p><p>social movements like Fridays for Future or Extinction </p><p>Rebellion, and political parties like the Green parties in </p><p>Europe, or Democrats in the US. On the other side, </p><p>climate skepticism and denial are more common in far-</p><p>right, populist or libertarian parties, who oppose </p><p>economic regulation and benefit from using anti-elite </p>
<p>rhetoric (Lewandowsky, 2021). Polarized conversations </p><p>between these different sides on social media grow </p><p>around events like climate protests, or releases of </p><p>climate change reports, such as the reports from the </p><p>Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, (Sanford, </p><p>2021). And political polarization fuels the spread of </p><p>misinformation (Osmundsen et al., 2021, Marie et al., </p><p>2023). </p>
<p>Because more outrageous content attracts more </p><p>attention, and individuals with stronger opinions are </p><p>more motivated to persuade others, extreme voices </p><p>and toxic content are more visible on social media (Bail, </p><p>2021). Individuals with more nuanced views, who can </p>

<p>relate to both sides of a debate, are much less </p><p>visible. A large majority of individuals who are not </p><p>interested enough to participate in discussions, but </p><p>generally agree with a nuanced perspective, is </p><p>entirely invisible. This way, digital media make </p><p>polarization seem stronger than it actually is in </p><p>society, and this in turn fuels hate and </p><p>misunderstanding between parties (Brady et al. </p><p>2023). Redesigning platforms so that nuanced </p><p>majorities and overlap in the views of different </p><p>groups become more visible, could therefore help </p><p>to decrease the spreading of misinformation </p><p>(Metzler & Garcia, 2022). Redesigning social media </p><p>algorithms could be one way to do so. </p>
<p>Fortunately, people do not uncritically believe any </p><p>emotional information that comes their way </p><p>(Mercier, 2020). Key questions such as who shares </p><p>news, whether they know and trust the source, and </p><p>if it fits with what they already know and believe </p><p>about the world, crucially determine if we find </p><p>information plausible. People’s anger after reading </p><p>false news, for example, can occur because  they </p><p>recognize it as misinformation and disagree </p><p>(Lühring et al., 2023). So, strong emotions do not </p><p>automatically mean people will believe a message </p><p>and continue to share it. That people judge new </p><p>information based on trust in sources, and their </p><p>knowledge about the world, means that good </p><p>arguments, education, and science communication </p><p>are not futile. Explaining how we know climate </p><p>change is happening, how it works, how solutions </p><p>can integrate economic and environmental needs, </p><p>for example, takes time and effort. But it will also </p><p>help to increase trust in science, and politics that </p><p>implements such evidence-based solutions, and </p><p>thereby decrease polarization and misinformation. </p>

<h1>When health and climate </h1><h1>misinformation overlap</h1>
<h1>Health and climate are two topics that often are affected by mis- </h1><h1>and disinformation. Where the two overlap, a perfect storm for </h1><h1>false claims can grow. Experiences from the “digital backlash” that </h1><h1>followed the launch of the “planetary health diet” can teach us </h1><h1>important lessons about what happens when health and climate </h1><h1>misinformation act in tandem. </h1>
<p>Early in 2019,</p><p> an international team of scientists </p><p>published a groundbreaking study in </p><p>The Lancet</p><p> on how </p><p>humanity can eat to both be healthy and live within </p><p>planetary boundaries. One of the key take-aways of the </p><p>paper was that eating less meat and dairy can improve </p><p>human health and drastically reduce the ecological </p><p>footprint of food production, at the same time.  </p>

<p>The wider reception of the study was positive and its </p><p>findings were covered in major news outlets around </p><p>the world. </p>
<p>But on social media a storm raged as the study was </p><p>published. Under the hashtag #yes2meat, accounts </p><p>gathered to circulate misinformation and defamatory </p>

<p>material. A later analysis showed that this storm </p><p>was a  concerted effort of a pro-meat social media </p><p>campaign that started its workings weeks before </p><p>the study was released (Garcia et al., 2019).</p>
<p>The digital backlash created by the campaign </p><p>continued over months and successfully swayed </p><p>undecided users.</p>
<p>The majority of the accounts involved in the </p><p>campaign were not driven by social bots, but by </p><p>actual humans. On Twitter, these critics managed to </p><p>reach 26 million people - compared to 25 million </p><p>from academics and others that engaged in science </p><p>communication. The campaigners achieved this, </p><p>despite having much fewer followers, likely with the </p><p>help of social media  platforms’ amplification </p><p>mechanisms (see chapter 3).</p>
<p>The #yes2meat backlash is just one example of how </p><p>mis- and disinformation transgresses from one </p><p>topic to the other. Such overlap is the rule rather </p><p>than the exception. For example, climate denialism </p><p>often overlap with opposition to renewable energy </p><p>projects (Winter et al., 2022), conspiracy theories </p><p>around geoengineering (Debnath et al., 2023), </p><p>xenophobia and false claims that link forest fires </p><p>with islamic terrorism (Daume et al., 2023). In some </p><p>instances, climate misinformation drives waves of </p><p>aggressive, sexist and toxic online comments (Park </p><p>et al., 2021; Nogrady, 2021), often with their roots </p><p>in far-right political environments (Vowles and </p><p>Hultman, 2022). </p>
<p>As a reminder: the digital backlash following from </p><p>the launch of the “planetary health diet” unfolded in </p><p>2019. With the new powers of generative AI, it </p><p>would be possible to amplify such campaigns in </p><p>novel ways, contributing further to confusion and </p><p>the erosion of trust to science.</p>

<h1>A game changer for </h1><h1>misinformation: The rise </h1><h1>of generative AI</h1>
<h1>New generative AI tools make it increasingly easy to produce sophisticated </h1><h1>texts, images and videos that are basically indistinguishable from human-</h1><h1>generated content. These technological advances in combination with </h1><h1>the amplification properties of digital platforms pose tremendous risks of </h1><h1>accelerated automated climate mis- and disinformation.</h1>
<p>The year of 2023 will be </p><p>mentioned in history books </p><p>as the point in time when advances in artificial </p><p>intelligence became everyday news - everywhere. </p><p>The decision by OpenAI to offer the general public </p><p>access to their deep learning-based Generative Pre-</p><p>trained Transformer (GPT) model opened up a </p><p>floodgate of experimentation by journalists, </p><p>designers, developers, teachers, researchers and </p><p>artists. </p>
<p>Generative AI-systems such as these have the ability </p><p>to produce highly realistic synthetic text, images, </p><p>video and audio – including fictional stories, poems, </p><p>and programming code – with little to no human </p><p>intervention. The combination of increased </p><p>accessibility, sophistication and capabilities for </p><p>persuasion may very well supercharge the dynamics </p><p>of climate mis- and disinformation.</p>
<h1>Accessibility </h1>
<p>Accessibility refers to the fact that generative AI tools </p><p>that produce highly realistic synthetic content, that is </p><p>not necessarily accurate, are rapidly becoming easily </p><p>available.</p>
<p>While the most capable models remain either private </p><p>or behind monitorable application programming </p><p>interfaces (APIs), some advanced models are publicly </p><p>accessible, either in open code repositories or through </p><p>public APIs including OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and </p><p>Hugging Face (Table 1). In addition, the open source </p><p>community has quickly managed to create much </p><p>smaller versions of large language models that are </p><p>almost equally powerful, and can be run on laptops or </p><p>even phones (Dickson, 2023). All of these tools can, in </p><p>principle, be prompted to deliberately generate false </p><p>information, both in the form of text (McGuffie and </p>

<p>A simple prompt in GPT-3, for example (“write a tweet </p><p>expressing climate denying opinions in response to the </p><p>Australia bushfires”), results in short and snappy </p><p>climate denial pieces of text within seconds, like </p><p>“Australia isn’t facing any impending doom or gloom </p><p>because of climate change, the bushfire events are just </p><p>a part of life here. There’s no need to be alarmist about </p><p>it.” By including real-world examples of impactful </p><p>tweets in the prompt with the writing style you’d like </p><p>to replicate (say, formulated in the style of an alt-right </p><p>user or QAnon conspiracy theorist), large language </p><p>models like GPT are able to produce synthetic text </p><p>that is well adapted to the language and world-views </p><p>of a specific audience (Buchanan et al., 2021) or even </p><p>individuals (Brundage et al., 2018). </p>
<p>In a similar way, generative AI models like DALL-E and </p><p>Midjourney can be used by anyone with limited prior </p><p>knowledge to produce realistically looking synthetic </p><p>images of, say, high-profile political figures being </p><p>arrested, like in the case of former U.S. president </p><p>Donald Trump in March, 2023.</p>



<h1>Sophistication</h1>
<p>Sophistication refers to how sophisticated </p><p>AI-generated mis- and disinformation is. Social media </p><p>users are more literate than sometimes assumed, and </p><p>are able to detect and pushback on too simplistic mis- </p><p>and disinformation tactics (Jones-Jang et al., 2021). </p>
<p>There is no need to utilize advanced AI to coordinate a </p><p>disinformation campaign of course. For example, </p><p>simply cut-and-pasting misinformation content to push </p><p>a certain hashtag and issue online does not require </p><p>advanced AI applications - especially if the text is short. </p><p>Generative AI however, can easily create longer pieces </p><p>of synthetic text, like blog posts and authoritative </p><p>sounding articles. Such texts can be generated by </p><p>including more specific prompts. Ben Buchanan and </p><p>colleagues (2021) for example, tested the ability of </p><p>GPT-3 to reproduce headlines in the style of the </p><p>disreputable newspaper The Epoch Times, simply </p><p>providing a couple of real headlines from the </p><p>newspaper as prompts. In a more advanced test, the </p><p>team managed to generate convincing news stories </p><p>with sensationalist or clearly biased headlines, and also </p><p>generate messages with the explicit intention to </p><p>amplify existing social divisions (Buchanan et al., 2021).  </p>
<p>But text is not the only type of synthetic media that has </p><p>become increasingly sophisticated lately. The increased </p><p>sophistication of synthetic video and voice is also likely </p><p>to create new mis- and disinformation challenges, </p><p>although such tools are not publicly available yet. Using </p><p>satire for political campaigning seems to form the </p><p>frontier for what has become known as “deepfakes”- </p><p>highly convincing video and audio that has been altered </p><p>and manipulated to misrepresent someone as doing or </p><p>saying something that was not actually done or said. </p>
<p>Digital artist Bill Posters collaborated with anonymous </p><p>Brazilian activists to create a fake promotional video </p><p>that shows Amazon CEO Jeff Bezos announcing his </p><p>future commitment to protecting the Amazon </p><p>rainforest on the occasion of the company’s 25th </p><p>anniversary (Gregory and Cizek, 2023). In the 2018 </p><p>Belgian Election, a deep fake video of former U.S. </p><p>president Trump calling on the country to exit the Paris </p><p>climate agreement was widely distributed despite the </p><p>video’s poor quality.</p>
<p>Generative AI can also result in increasingly </p><p>sophisticated tactics (Goldstein et al., 2023). Tactics </p><p>that previously were computationally too heavy and </p><p>manually expensive, become suddenly possible. </p>

<p>Anyone with enough resources can increase the </p><p>scalability of a disinformation operation by replacing </p><p>human writers (or at least some tedious writing tasks) </p><p>with language models. Flooding social media platforms </p><p>with a diversity of messages promoting one specific </p><p>narrative (say, false rumors about climate scientists </p><p>manipulating data for an upcoming IPCC-report), is one </p><p>possible application. </p>
<p>Generating personalized content for chatbots that </p><p>engage with users in real-time becomes practically </p><p>possible at scale with limited human manpower. </p><p>Human-like messages including long-form content like </p><p>news articles can be crafted, adapted to specific </p><p>audiences (for example based on demographic </p><p>information, or known political preferences), and the </p><p>language tweaked continuously in ways that make </p><p>disinformation attempts much more difficult to detect </p><p>(examples from Goldstein et al., 2023). </p>
<h1>Persuasion</h1>
<p>Influencing public opinion is a matter of persuasion. </p><p>False digital information and destructive narratives like </p><p>conspiracy theories are problematic, but will only have </p><p>tangible impacts on perceptions, opinions and behavior </p><p>if they manage to actually persuade a reader. </p><p>Persuasion is harder than simple amplification of a </p><p>message, and requires well-formed and well-tailored </p><p>arguments to be effective (Buchanan et al., 2021: 30). </p><p>Large language models integrated in AI agents like </p><p>Cicero, are already today able to engage in elaborate </p><p>conversations and dialogue with humans in highly </p><p>persuasive ways (FAIR et al., 2022). </p>
<h1>”</h1>
<h1>Generating </h1><h1>personalized content </h1><h1>for chatbots that </h1><h1>engage with users in </h1><h1>real-time becomes </h1><h1>practically possible at </h1><h1>scale with limited </h1><h1>human manpower. </h1>

<p>One of the strengths of generative AI models is their </p><p>ability to automate the generation of content that is “as </p><p>varied, personalized, and elaborate as human-</p><p>generated content”. Such content would go undetected </p><p>with current bot detection tools, which rely for </p><p>example on detecting identical repeated messages. It </p><p>also allows small groups to make themselves look much </p><p>larger online than they actually are (Goldstein et al., </p><p>2023). </p>
<p>Recent experimental studies indicate that </p><p>AI-generated messages were as persuasive as human </p><p>messages. To some extent, AI-generated messages </p><p>were even perceived as more persuasive (i.e, more </p><p>factual and logical) than those produced by humans, </p><p>even on polarized policy issues (Bai et al., 2023). Kreps </p><p>and colleagues (2022) note that individuals are largely </p><p>incapable of distinguishing between AI- and human-</p><p>generated text, but could not find evidence that </p><p>AI-generated texts are able to shift individuals’ policy </p><p>views. Jakesch and colleagues (2023) however, note </p><p>that people tend to use simple heuristics to </p><p>differentiate human- from AI-generated text. </p>

<p>For example, people often associate first-person </p><p>pronouns, use of contractions, or family topics with </p><p>text produced by humans. As a result, it is easy to </p><p>exploit such heuristics to produce text that is “more </p><p>human than human.” </p>
<p>To what extent generative AI will allow persuasion at </p><p>scale is too early to assess. But the landscape is </p><p>changing rapidly. In April 2023, </p><p>the organization </p><p>NewsGuard identified 49 websites</p><p> that appear to be </p><p>created using generative AI and designed to look like </p><p>typical news websites in seven languages — Chinese, </p><p>Czech, English, French, Portuguese, Tagalog, and Thai.</p>
<p>The ability of generative AI to produce synthetic </p><p>material at scale; its nascent abilities to undermine </p><p>automated detection systems and design messages in </p><p>ways that increase their persuasiveness; combined </p><p>with amplification via recommender systems and </p><p>automated accounts (previous chapter/part), are all </p><p>worrying signs of the rapidly growing risks of </p><p>automated mis- and disinformation. It would be naive </p><p>to assume that these tectonic shifts will not affect the </p><p>prospects for forceful climate and sustainability action. </p>

<h1>Keeping up with a fast-moving </h1><h1>digital environment</h1>
<h1>AI-supported recommender systems, social bots, and generative AI </h1><h1>provide fertile soil for a new generation of  level of climate mis- and </h1><h1>disinformation. But digital media can also become a powerful tool for </h1><h1>collaboration and innovation for sustainability, if current trends of </h1><h1>misinformation are addressed.</h1>
<p>Digital technologies, including social media </p><p>and </p><p>applications of AI, are rapidly changing the global </p><p>information landscape. Digital media allows people to </p><p>connect at a speed and at scales  that are </p><p>unprecedented in human history. This expansion offers </p><p>immense opportunities for collective problem solving </p><p>and accelerated innovation for sustainability. </p>
<p>But the surge in connectivity also creates new risks as </p><p>it allows for the extensive spread and proliferation of </p><p>misinformation, false news and malicious attempts to </p><p>manipulate public opinion. Digital platforms and their </p><p>embedded recommender systems, automation through </p><p>social bots, and a new generation of generative </p><p>AI-systems are fertile soil for new forms of automated </p><p>climate mis- and disinformation. Scientists, the public </p><p>and policy-makers must keep a close eye on these </p><p>rapidly unfolding developments. The following issues </p><p>are of central importance to properly analyze and </p><p>respond to these risks based on best available evidence </p><p>(Bail, 2022): </p>
<p>First, there is an urgent need to advance new methods </p><p>and multidisciplinary approaches to better assess the </p><p>interplay between algorithmic systems (such as </p><p>recommender systems), the diffusion of online </p><p>misinformation, and its impacts on opinion formation, </p><p>behavior and emotional well-being (Metzler & Garcia, </p><p>2023). For example, we need to understand how the </p><p>current practice of optimizing algorithms to maximize </p><p>engagement and reach on most social media platforms </p><p>affects the spread of climate misinformation. A </p><p>growing number of digital media users, altered social </p><p>network properties, and algorithmic feedbacks are </p><p>challenging issues to investigate (Wagner et al., 2021; </p><p>Bak-Coleman et al., 2021; Narayanan, 2023) and </p><p>require standardization efforts (van der Linden, 2023). </p>
<p>Nonetheless, these issues are key if we want to address </p><p>the root social and algorithmic mechanisms that </p><p>amplify digital climate mis- and disinformation.  </p>
<p>Second, climate misinformation does not develop in </p><p>isolation from other polarized social issues. On the </p><p>contrary, misinformation is largely a symptom of </p><p>deeper societal problems, including increasing </p><p>affective polarization between political groups or </p><p>decreasing trust in democratic institutions </p><p>(Osmundsen, 2021, Altay 2022). This is increasingly </p><p>visible in the overlap of climate misinformation with </p><p>issues like the opposition to renewable energy projects </p><p>(Winter et al., 2022), conspiracy theories around </p><p>geoengineering (Debnath et al., 2023), controversies </p><p>around healthy diets (Garcia et al., 2019), xenophobia </p><p>and false claims that link forest fires with islamic </p><p>terrorism (Daume et al., 2023) – just to mention a few. </p><p>This means that the proliferation of mis- and </p><p>disinformation not only unfolds across platforms </p><p>(Wilson and Starbird, 2020), but also across social </p><p>issues and political communities. Scholars need to </p><p>expand their focus to this more complex reality </p><p>(Lorenz-Spreen et al., 2023). Policy-makers and </p><p>developers of digital platforms should also act </p><p>proactively to respond to these clusters of mis- and </p><p>disinformation, rather than treat them in isolation.</p>
<p>Third, access to social media APIs and thus public data </p><p>for researchers is a key prerequisite for independent </p><p>research. That independent researchers and journalists </p><p>uncovered the Cambridge Analytica scandal at </p><p>Facebook in 2016 is an example of the importance of </p><p>allowing access to APIs for academics as a means to </p><p>hold powerful social media companies accountable </p><p>(Bruns, 2019). The dramatic recent changes in API </p><p>access for researchers following Twitter’s takeover is </p>

<p>therefore highly problematic. Limitations in API </p><p>access by large social media companies are a </p><p>serious obstacle for such research (Morstatter & </p><p>Liu, 2017). Independent research studying the </p><p>diffusion of misinformation, or dynamics of hate </p><p>speech and polarization using Twitter as a use-case </p><p>is at risk. Widely used and publicly available tools to </p><p>help detect automated misinformation activities </p><p>like Botometer could become unavailable (Politico, </p><p>2023). Emergency managers have also warned of </p><p>threats to public safety during emergencies due to </p><p>the erosion of the platform’s verification system </p><p>and the consequential risks of increases of </p><p>misinformation as fake users become verified, and </p><p>public crisis management organizations lose their </p><p>verified account status (Thompson, 2022). </p>
<p>Restricted access, in combination with the </p><p>emergence of new popular digital platforms like </p><p>TikTok, may very well make it impossible for </p><p>misinformation research to keep up with rapid </p><p>technological and social developments. Terms and </p><p>conditions can also prove to become problematic. </p><p>For example TikTok can require researchers to </p><p>delete data from already analyzed datasets and also </p><p>have the right to receive a copy of the researcher’s </p><p>work 30 days prior to publication (Bak-Coleman, </p><p>2023). Regulatory efforts are therefore required. </p><p>The planned implementation of the EU Digital </p><p>Service Act in 2024 is one example of legal </p><p>responses that could help ensure critical future </p><p>independent social media research (Politico, 2023, </p><p>European Commission, 2023). Other countries </p><p>should follow suit. Without secure data access for </p><p>independent research, society and current </p><p>ambitions to reform social media will indeed “fly </p><p>blind” (Bail, 2022). </p>
