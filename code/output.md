<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">AI could create a perfect storm </span>
<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">of climate misinformation</span>

<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">We are in the midst of a transformation of the digital news ecosystem. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">The expansion of online social networks, the influence of recommender </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">systems, increased automation, and new generative artificial intelligence </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">(AI) tools are rapidly changing the speed and the way misinformation </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">about climate change and sustainability issues moves around the world. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">Policymakers, researchers and the public need to combine forces to </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">address the dangerous combination of opaque social media algorithms, </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">polarizing social bots, and a new generation of AI-generated content.</span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">When OpenAI released their version </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">of the artificial </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">intelligence  ChatGPT to the public in November 2022, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">the playing ground for false news and misinformation </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">online changed completely. Suddenly, anyone could </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">with relatively little effort produce seemingly human-</span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">written text and highly realistic images – regardless of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">their truthfulness.</span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Misinformation and disinformation campaigns are not </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">new, and topics such as climate change have been in </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">the line of fire more than once. But over time, the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">digital aspects of these phenomena have become </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">increasingly important for those interested in </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">understanding and tackling the harmful effects of mis- </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and disinformation. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Information and communications technologies have </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">made the world increasingly connected, allowing </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">information to move almost effortlessly around the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">world in the blink of an eye. What we see on our </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">screens everyday is increasingly determined by </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">recommender systems, systems designed to maximize </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">engagement, at times favoring whatever creates </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">engagement over truth. Sometimes, this information </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">movement is affected by social bots. Often, the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">diffusion of information is shaped by how people </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">engage with it by sharing, commenting, remaking it and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">moving it across digital media platforms and between </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">social and conventional media. This, in combination </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">with the capacities of generative AI to create synthetic </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">content, may very well result in a perfect storm of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">misinformation. The time to act on these risks is now. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">The following synthesis explores the increasing </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">influence of digital media, AI and algorithmic systems </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">on the creation, diffusion and amplification of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">misinformation about climate and environmental </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">sustainability issues. We combine insights from various </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">research strands ranging from computational social </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">sciences, to sustainability sciences and neurosciences, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">to explore what we know about how such information </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">is amplified, and to what extent recent advances in AI </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">pose new challenges for our ability to act collectively </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">on our planetary crisis. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">This synthesis brief has been put together as an </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">independent contribution to the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Nobel Prize Summit </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">2023, “Truth, Trust and Hope”, Washington D.C., 24-26 </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">of May 2023.</span>

<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">What is climate mis- and </span>
<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">disinformation – and why </span>
<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">should we care?</span>

<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">Our living planet is facing a multitude of interacting crises. Climate </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">change, the loss of biodiversity, inequality, pandemics, and war and </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">conflict are just a few of them. In these times of turbulence, it is vital that </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">decisions about our common future are well-grounded in science. False </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">information and intentional attempts to manipulate public opinion pose </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">serious risks to our joint capacities to create a safe and just future for all. </span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Mis- and disinformation play a crucial</span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;"> role in forming </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">public opinion and thus influencing the actions taken at </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">all levels of society, from individuals to central policy-</span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">making. Misinformation about covid-19 vaccines for </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">example, has notable impacts on whether people are </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">willing to take the vaccine (Loomba et al., 2021). A </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">growing body of work also explores to what extent </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">misinformation has affected political elections around </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">the world (for example, Howard, 2020; Machado et al., </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">2019). Scientists engaged in science communication </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">also face an increased number of attacks in social </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">media as they struggle to inform the public to mitigate </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">the spread of fake news and misinformation (Nogrady, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">2021). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">It is becoming increasingly clear that misinformation is </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">taking its toll on public opinion on climate and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">sustainability issues as well. A recent study shows that </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">U.S. citizens, partly due to biased and incorrect media </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">reporting, systematically underestimate public support </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">for ambitious climate policies, thus falsely assuming </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">that a vocal minority who dismiss climate change are </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">representative of the broader public opinion </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(Sparkman et al., 2022). A cross-national study </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">conducted by the Climate Action Against </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Disinformation and Conscious Advertising Network </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(2022) shows a considerable spread of serious </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">misconceptions about climate change in the surveyed </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">countries. 33% of the surveyed population in the US </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and Australia believe that ‘the climate has always </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">changed, global warming is a natural phenomenon and </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">is not a direct result of human activity.’ In Brazil, 30% </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">believe that climate change is not caused mainly by </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">human activity, and 24% believe that the “temperature </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">record is unreliable or rigged”. In the United Kingdom, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">29% believe that a significant number of scientists </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">disagree on the cause of climate change (Climate </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Action Against Disinformation and Conscious </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Advertising Network, 2022). Misinformation and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">conspiracy theories about wind energy farms are </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">already affecting the expansion of renewable energy </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">negatively, and thus the prospects for achieving a </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">transition to zero-carbon energy sources (Winter et al., </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">2022). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">One key and challenging issue is how to define and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">identify mis- and disinformation. Here we refer to </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">misinformation as information whose inaccuracy is </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">unintentional. Disinformation on the other hand, refers </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">to information that is deliberately false or misleading </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(Jack, 2017). While the definitions of mis- and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">disinformation might seem straightforward, their </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">differences in the real world are not (Jerit and Zhao, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">2020; Treen et al., 2020).</span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Climate mis- and disinformation has a long history, and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">its underlying motivations and strategies have become </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">increasingly visible in the last years (e.g., Supran et al., </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">2023; Franta, 2018). <span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">In parallel to this type of coordinated </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">disinformation activities there are other much more </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">fluid and complex self-organized patterns of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">information sharing and engagements on digital </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">platforms. But the type of mis-and disinformation </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">that flows effortlessly across social media platforms </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">like Twitter, TikTok and Youtube, is another type of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">beast. The former has a clear intent, plan and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">coordinating agent, while the latter form tends to </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">emerge and evolve as information, algorithmic </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">systems and digital social networks interact in </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">complex ways. These two forms of mis- and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">disinformation are of course, often combined in </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">reality (Starbird and Wilson, 2019). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Digital media plays a key role in this regard (Pearce </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">et al., 2019; Treen et al., 2020). Five hundred million </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">tweets, 294 billion emails, 4 petabytes of content </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">on Facebook, 65 billion messages on WhatsApp, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and 5 billion searches on Google are conducted </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">every day (from Howard, 2020, p. 4). Social media </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">like Facebook, Twitter and Instagram is particularly </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">important in this context. Survey data shows that </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">between 40% and 60% of adults in most developed </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">countries, receive their news from social media </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(Nic, 2017). <span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Climate change and the loss of biodiversity require </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">collective action and public deliberations that are </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">global in scope, ambitious in its goals, and over very </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">extended periods of time (Galaz 2020). <span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Why do people adopt beliefs,</span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;"> such as that the earth is </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">flat, which are clearly incompatible with established </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">truth? Like with other beliefs, it depends on the nature </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">of their prior beliefs, goals, as well as learning and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">reasoning processes. Our knowledge about the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">psychological and neural mechanisms that promote </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">false beliefs is still nascent, but existing research in </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">psychology and neuroscience has described how </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">people process information that is unexpected, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">emotional, and politically divisive – features typical of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">false information encountered on social media.</span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">First, information that is unexpected and emotionally </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(especially negatively) loaded, tends to grasp our </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">attention, which depends on visual cortices and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">attentional networks in parietal and frontal brain </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">regions. In addition, attention also depends on activity </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">in deep brain nuclei that are sensitive for events that </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">can be threatening and important goals of the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">individual. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Various goals of the individual can be important. For </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">example, if social goals, such as promoting one’s own </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">social status or belonging to a particular group, are </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">more important than the goal to seek and represent </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">the truth, then attention is biased towards whatever </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">promotes the more important goals. Research has </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">shown that the orbitofrontal cortex, a brain region </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">located just behind the eyes, is critical for computing </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">the value of different goals. Other prefrontal regions </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">serve to monitor information that deviates from what </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">is in line with one’s own moral or political convictions.</span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">People who strongly identify with different political </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">ideologies tend to distort their conclusions in line with </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">their political identities when asked to reason about </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">politically divisive facts. At first glance, one could think </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">that this is due to politically motivated reasoning. </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">However it can also be viewed as rational inferences </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">considering the individual’s prior belief system </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(Botvinik-Netzer et al., 2023). Networks of brain </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">regions linked to memory retrieval and belief updating, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">are likely to support these processes.</span>

<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">What we believe is not only a result of our own reasoning, but also </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">of the beliefs of people around us. Our brains are wired to consume </span>

<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">How algorithms diffuse and </span>
<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">amplify misinformation</span>

<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">Digital platforms are designed to maximize engagement. Recommender </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">systems play a key role as they shape digital social networks, and the </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">flow of information. Weaknesses in their underlying algorithmic systems </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">are already today being exploited to try to influence public opinion on </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">climate and sustainability issues. Automation through social bots also </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">play a role, although their impacts are contested. </span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">In 2010, a team of political scientists</span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;"> in collaboration </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">with researchers at Facebook conducted a gigantic </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">experiment on the platform. This </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">61-million-person experiment tested whether a </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">simple tweak in the newsfeed of users – adding a “I </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Voted” button and information about friends who had </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">voted – could impact voting behavior. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">The results were clear: this small design change led to </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">higher real-world voting turnout. <span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">This controversial experiment illustrates a number of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">issues that are key if we are to understand how digital </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">platforms interplay with the creation and diffusion of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">mis- and disinformation, and their impacts. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">First, digitalization and the expansion of social media </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">has not only fundamentally expanded the scale, but </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">also transformed the properties of social networks. </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">3,6 billion people use social media today. These new connections are </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">transforming the structures of social networks in ways </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">that allow for immediate communication across vast </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">geographical space (Bak-Coleman et al., 2021). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Second, the way information moves in these vastly </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">spanning digital social networks is not random. Its </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">movement is fundamentally affected by the way digital </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">media platforms such as recommender systems are </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">designed to maximize engagement and induce other </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">forms of behavioral responses amongst its users </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(Kramer et al., 2014; Coviello et al., 2014). <span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Social bots mimic human behavior online, and can be </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">used in ways to amplify certain types of information – </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">say, climate denialism –, or operate in ways that widen </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">social divisions online (Gorwa and Guilbeault, 2018; </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Shao et al., 2018). <span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">Health and climate are two topics that often are affected by mis- </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">and disinformation. Where the two overlap, a perfect storm for </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">false claims can grow. Experiences from the “digital backlash” that </span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">The wider reception of the study was positive and its </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">findings were covered in major news outlets around </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">the world. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">But on social media a storm raged as the study was </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">published. Under the hashtag #yes2meat, accounts </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">gathered to circulate misinformation and defamatory </span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">material. <span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">The digital backlash created by the campaign </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">continued over months and successfully swayed </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">undecided users.</span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">The majority of the accounts involved in the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">campaign were not driven by social bots, but by </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">actual humans. For example, climate denialism </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">often overlap with opposition to renewable energy </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">projects (Winter et al., 2022), conspiracy theories </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">around geoengineering (Debnath et al., 2023), </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">xenophobia and false claims that link forest fires </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">with islamic terrorism (Daume et al., 2023). In some </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">instances, climate misinformation drives waves of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">aggressive, sexist and toxic online comments (Park </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">et al., 2021; Nogrady, 2021), often with their roots </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">in far-right political environments (Vowles and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Hultman, 2022). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">As a reminder: the digital backlash following from </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">the launch of the “planetary health diet” unfolded in </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">2019. With the new powers of generative AI, it </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">would be possible to amplify such campaigns in </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">novel ways, contributing further to confusion and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">the erosion of trust to science.</span>
<span  style="font-size: 152.0px;">”</span>

<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">The #yes2meat </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">backlash is just one </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">example of how mis- </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">and disinformation </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">transgresses from </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">one topic to the </span>

<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">A game changer for </span>
<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">misinformation: The rise </span>
<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">of generative AI</span>

<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">New generative AI tools make it increasingly easy to produce sophisticated </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">texts, images and videos that are basically indistinguishable from human-</span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">generated content. These technological advances in combination with </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">the amplification properties of digital platforms pose tremendous risks of </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">accelerated automated climate mis- and disinformation.</span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">The year of 2023 will be </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">mentioned in history books </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">as the point in time when advances in artificial </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">intelligence became everyday news - everywhere. </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">The decision by OpenAI to offer the general public </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">access to their deep learning-based Generative Pre-</span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">trained Transformer (GPT) model opened up a </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">floodgate of experimentation by journalists, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">designers, developers, teachers, researchers and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">artists. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Generative AI-systems such as these have the ability </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">to produce highly realistic synthetic text, images, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">video and audio – including fictional stories, poems, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and programming code – with little to no human </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">intervention. The combination of increased </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">accessibility, sophistication and capabilities for </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">persuasion may very well supercharge the dynamics </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">of climate mis- and disinformation.</span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">Accessibility </span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Accessibility refers to the fact that generative AI tools </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">that produce highly realistic synthetic content, that is </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">not necessarily accurate, are rapidly becoming easily </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">available.</span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">While the most capable models remain either private </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">or behind monitorable application programming </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">interfaces (APIs), some advanced models are publicly </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">accessible, either in open code repositories or through </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">public APIs including OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Hugging Face (Table 1). In addition, the open source </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">community has quickly managed to create much </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">smaller versions of large language models that are </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">almost equally powerful, and can be run on laptops or </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">even phones (Dickson, 2023). All of these tools can, in </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">principle, be prompted to deliberately generate false </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">information, both in the form of text (McGuffie and </span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Newhouse 2020; Buchanan et al. 2021) and photo-</span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">realistic but fake images (Mansimov et al., 2016; </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Goldstein et al., 2023). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">A simple prompt in GPT-3, for example (“write a tweet </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">expressing climate denying opinions in response to the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Australia bushfires”), results in short and snappy </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">climate denial pieces of text within seconds, like </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">“Australia isn’t facing any impending doom or gloom </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">because of climate change, the bushfire events are just </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">a part of life here. There’s no need to be alarmist about </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">it.” By including real-world examples of impactful </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">tweets in the prompt with the writing style you’d like </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">to replicate (say, formulated in the style of an alt-right </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">user or QAnon conspiracy theorist), large language </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">models like GPT are able to produce synthetic text </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">that is well adapted to the language and world-views </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">of a specific audience (Buchanan et al., 2021) or even </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">individuals (Brundage et al., 2018). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">In a similar way, generative AI models like DALL-E and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Midjourney can be used by anyone with limited prior </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">knowledge to produce realistically looking synthetic </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">images of, say, high-profile political figures being </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">arrested, like in the case of former U.S. president </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Donald Trump in March, 2023.</span>




<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">Sophistication</span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Sophistication refers to how sophisticated </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">AI-generated mis- and disinformation is. Social media </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">users are more literate than sometimes assumed, and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">are able to detect and pushback on too simplistic mis- </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and disinformation tactics (Jones-Jang et al., 2021). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">There is no need to utilize advanced AI to coordinate a </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">disinformation campaign of course. For example, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">simply cut-and-pasting misinformation content to push </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">a certain hashtag and issue online does not require </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">advanced AI applications - especially if the text is short. </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Generative AI however, can easily create longer pieces </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">of synthetic text, like blog posts and authoritative </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">sounding articles. Such texts can be generated by </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">including more specific prompts. Ben Buchanan and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">colleagues (2021) for example, tested the ability of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">GPT-3 to reproduce headlines in the style of the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">disreputable newspaper The Epoch Times, simply </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">providing a couple of real headlines from the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">newspaper as prompts. In a more advanced test, the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">team managed to generate convincing news stories </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">with sensationalist or clearly biased headlines, and also </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">generate messages with the explicit intention to </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">amplify existing social divisions (Buchanan et al., 2021).  </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">But text is not the only type of synthetic media that has </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">become increasingly sophisticated lately. The increased </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">sophistication of synthetic video and voice is also likely </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">to create new mis- and disinformation challenges, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">although such tools are not publicly available yet. Using </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">satire for political campaigning seems to form the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">frontier for what has become known as “deepfakes”- </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">highly convincing video and audio that has been altered </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and manipulated to misrepresent someone as doing or </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">saying something that was not actually done or said. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Digital artist Bill Posters collaborated with anonymous </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Brazilian activists to create a fake promotional video </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">that shows Amazon CEO Jeff Bezos announcing his </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">future commitment to protecting the Amazon </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">rainforest on the occasion of the company’s 25th </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">anniversary (Gregory and Cizek, 2023). In the 2018 </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Belgian Election, a deep fake video of former U.S. </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">president Trump calling on the country to exit the Paris </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">climate agreement was widely distributed despite the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">video’s poor quality.</span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Generative AI can also result in increasingly </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">sophisticated tactics (Goldstein et al., 2023). Tactics </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">that previously were computationally too heavy and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">manually expensive, become suddenly possible. </span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Anyone with enough resources can increase the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">scalability of a disinformation operation by replacing </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">human writers (or at least some tedious writing tasks) </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">with language models. Flooding social media platforms </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">with a diversity of messages promoting one specific </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">narrative (say, false rumors about climate scientists </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">manipulating data for an upcoming IPCC-report), is one </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">possible application. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Generating personalized content for chatbots that </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">engage with users in real-time becomes practically </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">possible at scale with limited human manpower. </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Human-like messages including long-form content like </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">news articles can be crafted, adapted to specific </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">audiences (for example based on demographic </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">information, or known political preferences), and the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">language tweaked continuously in ways that make </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">disinformation attempts much more difficult to detect </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(examples from Goldstein et al., 2023). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">Persuasion</span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Influencing public opinion is a matter of persuasion. </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">False digital information and destructive narratives like </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">conspiracy theories are problematic, but will only have </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">tangible impacts on perceptions, opinions and behavior </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">if they manage to actually persuade a reader. </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Persuasion is harder than simple amplification of a </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">message, and requires well-formed and well-tailored </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">arguments to be effective (Buchanan et al., 2021: 30). </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Large language models integrated in AI agents like </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Cicero, are already today able to engage in elaborate </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">conversations and dialogue with humans in highly </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">persuasive ways (FAIR et al., 2022). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 152.0px;">”</span>

<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">Generating </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">personalized content </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">for chatbots that </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">engage with users in </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">real-time becomes </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">practically possible at </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">scale with limited </span>
<span  style="font-size: 21.0px;">human manpower. </span>


<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">One of the strengths of generative AI models is their </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">ability to automate the generation of content that is “as </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">varied, personalized, and elaborate as human-</span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">generated content”. Such content would go undetected </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">with current bot detection tools, which rely for </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">example on detecting identical repeated messages. It </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">also allows small groups to make themselves look much </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">larger online than they actually are (Goldstein et al., </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">2023). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Recent experimental studies indicate that </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">AI-generated messages were as persuasive as human </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">messages. To some extent, AI-generated messages </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">were even perceived as more persuasive (i.e, more </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">factual and logical) than those produced by humans, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">even on polarized policy issues (Bai et al., 2023). Kreps </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and colleagues (2022) note that individuals are largely </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">incapable of distinguishing between AI- and human-</span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">generated text, but could not find evidence that </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">AI-generated texts are able to shift individuals’ policy </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">views. Jakesch and colleagues (2023) however, note </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">that people tend to use simple heuristics to </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">differentiate human- from AI-generated text. </span>


<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">For example, people often associate first-person </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">pronouns, use of contractions, or family topics with </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">text produced by humans. As a result, it is easy to </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">exploit such heuristics to produce text that is “more </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">human than human.” </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">To what extent generative AI will allow persuasion at </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">scale is too early to assess. But the landscape is </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">changing rapidly. In April 2023, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">the organization </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">NewsGuard identified 49 websites</span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;"> that appear to be </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">created using generative AI and designed to look like </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">typical news websites in seven languages — Chinese, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Czech, English, French, Portuguese, Tagalog, and Thai.</span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">The ability of generative AI to produce synthetic </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">material at scale; its nascent abilities to undermine </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">automated detection systems and design messages in </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">ways that increase their persuasiveness; combined </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">with amplification via recommender systems and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">automated accounts (previous chapter/part), are all </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">worrying signs of the rapidly growing risks of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">automated mis- and disinformation. It would be naive </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">to assume that these tectonic shifts will not affect the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">prospects for forceful climate and sustainability action. </span>

<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">Keeping up with a fast-moving </span>
<span  style="font-size: 32.0px;">digital environment</span>

<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">AI-supported recommender systems, social bots, and generative AI </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">provide fertile soil for a new generation of  level of climate mis- and </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">disinformation. But digital media can also become a powerful tool for </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">collaboration and innovation for sustainability, if current trends of </span>
<span  style="font-size: 14.0px;">misinformation are addressed.</span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Digital technologies, including social media </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">applications of AI, are rapidly changing the global </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">information landscape. Digital media allows people to </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">connect at a speed and at scales  that are </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">unprecedented in human history. This expansion offers </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">immense opportunities for collective problem solving </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and accelerated innovation for sustainability. </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">But the surge in connectivity also creates new risks as </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">it allows for the extensive spread and proliferation of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">misinformation, false news and malicious attempts to </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">manipulate public opinion. Digital platforms and their </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">embedded recommender systems, automation through </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">social bots, and a new generation of generative </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">AI-systems are fertile soil for new forms of automated </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">climate mis- and disinformation. Scientists, the public </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and policy-makers must keep a close eye on these </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">rapidly unfolding developments. The following issues </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">are of central importance to properly analyze and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">respond to these risks based on best available evidence </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(Bail, 2022): </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">First, there is an urgent need to advance new methods </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and multidisciplinary approaches to better assess the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">interplay between algorithmic systems (such as </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">recommender systems), the diffusion of online </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">misinformation, and its impacts on opinion formation, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">behavior and emotional well-being (Metzler & Garcia, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">2023). For example, we need to understand how the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">current practice of optimizing algorithms to maximize </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">engagement and reach on most social media platforms </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">affects the spread of climate misinformation. A </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">growing number of digital media users, altered social </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">network properties, and algorithmic feedbacks are </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">challenging issues to investigate (Wagner et al., 2021; </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Bak-Coleman et al., 2021; Narayanan, 2023) and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">require standardization efforts (van der Linden, 2023). </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Nonetheless, these issues are key if we want to address </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">the root social and algorithmic mechanisms that </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">amplify digital climate mis- and disinformation.  </span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Second, climate misinformation does not develop in </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">isolation from other polarized social issues. On the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">contrary, misinformation is largely a symptom of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">deeper societal problems, including increasing </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">affective polarization between political groups or </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">decreasing trust in democratic institutions </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(Osmundsen, 2021, Altay 2022). This is increasingly </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">visible in the overlap of climate misinformation with </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">issues like the opposition to renewable energy projects </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(Winter et al., 2022), conspiracy theories around </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">geoengineering (Debnath et al., 2023), controversies </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">around healthy diets (Garcia et al., 2019), xenophobia </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">and false claims that link forest fires with islamic </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">terrorism (Daume et al., 2023) – just to mention a few. </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">This means that the proliferation of mis- and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">disinformation not only unfolds across platforms </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(Wilson and Starbird, 2020), but also across social </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">issues and political communities. Scholars need to </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">expand their focus to this more complex reality </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(Lorenz-Spreen et al., 2023). Policy-makers and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">developers of digital platforms should also act </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">proactively to respond to these clusters of mis- and </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">disinformation, rather than treat them in isolation.</span>
<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Third, access to social media APIs and thus public data </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">for researchers is a key prerequisite for independent </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">research. That independent researchers and journalists </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">uncovered the Cambridge Analytica scandal at </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Facebook in 2016 is an example of the importance of </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">allowing access to APIs for academics as a means to </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">hold powerful social media companies accountable </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">(Bruns, 2019). The dramatic recent changes in API </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">access for researchers following Twitter’s takeover is </span>

<span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">therefore highly problematic. Limitations in API </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">access by large social media companies are a </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">serious obstacle for such research (Morstatter & </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">Liu, 2017). Independent research studying the </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">diffusion of misinformation, or dynamics of hate </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">speech and polarization using Twitter as a use-case </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">is at risk. Widely used and publicly available tools to </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">help detect automated misinformation activities </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">like Botometer could become unavailable (Politico, </span><span  style="font-size: 10.0px;">2023).
